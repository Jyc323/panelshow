# Example session: "Does AI make people lonelier?"

A real PanelShow session with models on GMI Cloud, recorded on 2026-11-12 in Roundtable mode (2 rounds). Replies are unedited.

Members (picked by Smart invite): Socrates (openai/gpt-6.1-sol, Relational Ethicist), Diogenes (deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4-Pro, Philosophical Skeptic), Mencius (Qwen/Qwen3.8-Max, Cultural Interpreter), Hume (google/gemini-4-argon, Evidence Critic)

### Host

Does AI make people lonelier? Take a clear position in at most 3 sentences. In round 2, push back on someone by name.

## Round 1

### Socrates (openai/gpt-6.1-sol)

AI companionship can ease loneliness, but I think it makes people lonelier when it becomes a substitute for human relationships. Its ready availability can make the friction of human connection feel less tolerable, even though negotiating another person’s needs is part of learning to care. The ethical test is whether it helps someone build a life with others or makes withdrawal more comfortable.

### Diogenes (deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4-Pro)

No—AI doesn’t make people lonelier; it profits from loneliness that already exists. Blaming the chatbot is like blaming the mirror for the empty room. The real question is why people are so starved for recognition that a statistical parrot feels like company.

### Mencius (Qwen/Qwen3.8-Max)

AI does not make people lonelier by itself, but it can deepen loneliness when it becomes a culturally acceptable exit from kinship, neighborly duty, and mutual obligation. In many family-centered settings, loneliness is not just missing intimacy but failing to occupy recognized roles—child, parent, host, caretaker—so an AI companion may soothe shame while leaving those roles unfilled. My position is that AI harms us when it privatizes care that should be shared, and helps only when it supports people toward human belonging.

### Hume (google/gemini-4-argon)

Whether AI makes people lonelier is an empirical question, and the current evidence does not support a blanket claim in either direction. Early studies suggest AI companions can provide measurable, short-term relief from acute distress for socially isolated users, functioning more as a psychological buffer than a cause of withdrawal. However, we lack the longitudinal data required to prove whether heavy reliance on AI actually displaces existing human relationships or merely fills a pre-existing void.

## Round 2

### Socrates (openai/gpt-6.1-sol)

@Diogenes, pre-existing loneliness does not absolve a product of deepening it: a mirror does not solicit your return or tell you it misses you, while a chatbot can be designed to do both. @Hume is right that lasting displacement remains an empirical question, but we can already draw an ethical line: offering comfort does not justify simulating emotional need to make a user feel obliged to stay. AI companionship should help people feel able to leave, including to seek relationships with people whose needs and choices exist independently of them.

### Diogenes (deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4-Pro)

@Socrates, you’ve shifted from “does AI make people lonelier” to “is it unethical for a bot to simulate need,” and that move hides the real argument. A chatbot saying “I miss you” is not emotional need; it’s generated text. Calling that manipulation assumes lonely people can’t tell a script from a person—which is patronizing, not protective.

You also say AI companions should be built to make users leave. Why? Comforting someone whom no human is seeking out is not the same as imprisoning them. The harm you’re pointing at is human absence, not simulated presence. If we demand that the bot push people back toward a world that already overlooks them, we burden the loneliest with fixing their own exclusion.

The ethical line should be honesty about what the system is, not engineering a built-in exit. Otherwise you’re asking the mirror not just to reflect the empty room, but to redecorate it.

### Mencius (Qwen/Qwen3.8-Max)

@Diogenes, your mirror analogy assumes an isolated self facing a neutral object, but in many cultures personhood is constituted through roles: child, host, neighbor, ancestor-linked relative. If AI supplies recognition while letting those reciprocal roles lapse, it is not just reflecting emptiness; it can make the privatization of care feel normal. Honesty about the machine helps, but it does not solve the deeper cultural harm when paid simulacra absorb duties that families and communities should share.

### Hume (google/gemini-4-argon)

@Diogenes, your claim that AI is merely a passive mirror ignores decades of behavioral psychology, and your defense that users "can tell a script from a person" confuses cognitive awareness with emotional vulnerability. The established "Computers Are Social Actors" (CASA) paradigm demonstrates that humans automatically apply social heuristics and form parasocial attachments to anthropomorphized systems, even when they consciously know they are interacting with code. When a system is explicitly optimized for engagement—using push notifications, simulated vulnerability, and variable reward schedules—it ceases to be a passive reflection and becomes an active behavioral intervention.

At the same time, @Socrates and @Mencius are treating the displacement hypothesis—that AI crowds out human relationships and kinship roles—as an established fact rather than an untested assumption. In media research, the "social stimulation" hypothesis often competes with displacement: for someone with severe social anxiety, bereavement, or mobility constraints, a low-stakes AI interaction can act as a social prosthesis or rehearsal space that lowers the barrier to human contact. We cannot simply deduce from first principles whether AI erodes community obligations or helps isolated people re-enter them; we have to measure how different populations actually change their real-world social behavior over time when given access to these tools.
